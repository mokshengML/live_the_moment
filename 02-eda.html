Exploratory Data Analysis — Lesson 02
No-Code Interactive Lesson

Make data tell you what's inside before you model it

EDA is what every great analyst does before running a single algorithm: look at the data, find its shape, spot the weird stuff. By the end of this lesson you'll know how to read a histogram, a boxplot, and a correlation matrix the way a doctor reads an X-ray.

🏃

The Case: "FitPath" — 500 users, one big question

A fitness app team wants to know: "Why are some users churning?" Before building a fancy churn model, you'll inspect their data — daily steps, sleep, workout minutes, calories, age — and let the patterns surface themselves. This is exactly how real data scientists open a new dataset.

1
Distributions — the "shape" of a column

Every numeric column has a personality: some are tightly bunched (most users sleep ~7 hours), some are spread (income often is), some have weird humps (two types of users hiding in one column). Distribution is the word for that personality. Knowing it changes how you analyse, summarise, and even what model you choose.

Analogy: If columns are people, the distribution is their shape — tall and narrow (concentrated), short and wide (spread out), or hunchbacked (two groups in disguise).

What to notice: When mean ≠ median, the distribution is skewed — averages will mislead you. Always look at the shape before quoting an "average".
2
Histograms — counting friends in each height bracket

A histogram chops a numeric column into buckets ("bins") and counts how many rows fall into each. Easy to read, but there's a trap: too few bins hide the shape, too many bins show only noise. The right bin width is a craft, not a setting.

Analogy: You're sorting jellybeans by weight into cups. Use 3 huge cups → you'll lose detail. Use 100 tiny cups → most will be empty and you can't see the pattern. The sweet spot is where the story emerges.
▶ Try it — Slide the bin count and watch the story change

This is FitPath users' weekly workout minutes. Slide the bins. At 5 bins you'll see "people work out somewhere between 0 and 600 min." At 50 you'll see the actual pattern: two groups — casual users (~50 min) and serious users (~300 min). The same column tells different stories.

15
Read this carefully: The bumps you see in the middle bin counts represent real groups of users with different behaviours. If you'd only used 5 bins, you would have missed them and recommended a single workout-time strategy for everyone.
3
Boxplots — the X-ray of a column

Boxplots compress a whole distribution into one tiny diagram: the middle box is where the typical half of your data lives, the line inside is the median, the whiskers show the rest, and any lonely dots are outliers — values so far from normal that they deserve a second look. Once you can read one, you can compare 10 groups side-by-side at a glance.

Analogy: A boxplot is like a class photo line-up sorted by height — you instantly see who's average, who's tall, who's short, and the one kid standing on a chair to look taller (the outlier).
▶ Try it — Compare workout minutes across user segments

Hover the boxes. Each box is one segment of users (Free, Basic, Premium, Elite). Where's the median highest? Which segment has wild outliers? This is how product managers identify "power users" worth retaining.

What boxplots reveal: Elite users have the highest median and tightest box — they're consistently active. Free users have a long bottom whisker — many barely use the app. Outliers above Premium might be Elite-tier users on the wrong plan (a growth opportunity).
4
Correlation matrix — which columns move together?

Correlation answers a simple question: when column A goes up, does column B tend to go up too? It runs from −1 (perfectly opposite) through 0 (no relationship) to +1 (perfectly together). A correlation matrix shows this for every pair of columns at once — a single grid that maps the "internal politics" of your dataset.

Analogy: Imagine your dataset's columns are colleagues. Correlation tells you which ones always show up at the same meetings. Strong positive = best friends. Strong negative = sworn rivals. Near zero = they don't even know each other.
▶ Try it — Hover the grid for plain-English readings

Each cell shows how strongly two FitPath columns relate. Dark orange = strong positive. Dark navy = strong negative. White-ish = no real link. Important warning: correlation ≠ causation. We'll see why below.

Spurious correlations: Notice "App opens" and "Workout minutes" correlate 0.78. Causal? Probably — people who open the app actually work out. But "Sleep hours" and "Calorie intake" correlate 0.40 too. Does sleeping make you eat more? Or do older users (a third variable) both sleep more and eat more? Correlation flags the question. Causation needs a test.
5
Pair plots — every scatter, all at once

A pair plot is a grid of mini scatter plots: one for every pair of numeric columns. It's the fastest possible "scan the dataset" tool — clusters, outliers, curves, and segments all show up at a glance. Data scientists run this on every new dataset before doing anything else.

Analogy: A pair plot is the contact sheet a photographer prints before deciding which photo to enlarge. Tiny thumbnails of every pairing — you scan the whole roll, then zoom into the interesting ones.
▶ Try it — Colour by user segment, scan for patterns

Each tiny plot shows one pair of columns. Dots are users. Colour by their segment — if the colours separate in a plot, that pair of columns is great for predicting segment. If colours overlap completely, those columns won't help.

What to look for: When colouring by churn, the (Workout-min, App-opens) plot in the top-right shows clear separation — churned users cluster in the bottom-left (low activity). That's a strong early signal — you've already done feature selection without writing a single line of code.

🌱 Five tools, one habit: look before you model

Every great analyst runs these checks before touching a model. They take minutes, save weeks, and often reveal that you don't need a model at all — the answer was hiding in the histogram.

Distribution

The personality of a column. Bell, skewed, multi-peak.

Histogram

How many rows fall in each value range. Mind the bin count.

Boxplot

Median, spread, outliers — perfect for comparing groups.

Correlation matrix

Which column-pairs move together. Never assume causation.

Pair plot
