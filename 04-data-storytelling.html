Make the numbers talk

You can have the cleanest dataset, the smartest model, the most accurate chart — and still lose the room. Storytelling is the skill that turns analysis into action. In this lesson you'll edit a real chart, sequence a narrative, and build a 60-second script for three different audiences.

The Case: FreshFare Grocery — Q3 satisfaction is sinking

You're an analyst at FreshFare, a 30-minute grocery delivery startup. Last quarter your 5-star rating share dropped from 64% to 49%. The CEO has booked 15 minutes on her calendar — "tell me what's going on and what to do about it." The data is ready. Now you have to tell the story.

1
Why stories beat charts

A chart shows the data. A story tells the listener what to do about it. The same chart can leave a room confused or trigger a decision in 30 seconds — depending on how it's framed.

Think of it like this: Giving someone a chart with no commentary is like handing them a map with no destination. They'll squint at the streets and ask, "What am I looking for?" Your job as an analyst is to circle the destination and say, "We need to go here. This route is fastest."

2
Annotated visualisations — turn the lights on, one switch at a time

A great chart is built in layers: the data, then the framing, then the call-outs. Most analysts stop at layer one. Here's how each annotation adds a new sentence to your story. Toggle them on and watch the chart speak louder.

Annotation = sentence. Each one is a thing you'd say out loud. If you have to say something to make the chart clear ("ignore April, it's an outlier", "this dip is when we launched the new app"), put it on the chart. Your future self in a slack message will thank you.
▸ Build the chart one annotation at a time
1Clear, opinionated title
Not "Sales by month" — say what's interesting about it.
2Highlight the bar that matters
Grey out the rest. Eyes go where the colour is.
3Add a callout with the "why"
A label, an arrow, a tiny explanation of the spike or dip.
4Spell out the takeaway
One line at the bottom: what should the viewer DO?
You're showing the raw chart. A viewer can guess that something happened with cold-chain — but they have to do the work. Add at least the title and the highlight to lift them out of that work.
3
EDA narrative — the five-card story arc

Every good analysis follows the same shape. Whether you write a memo, a slide deck or a research paper, you're telling some version of: we wondered → we looked → we found → it matters because → so let's...

Movies do this too. Setup, inciting incident, investigation, revelation, resolution. Your analysis IS a detective story. Skipping a beat is why presentations leave people asking, "Sorry, what's the point?"

What you just practised

You moved from "showing data" to "telling someone what to do about it" — the single biggest career skill in analytics. The same pattern works for a research paper, a board memo or a Slack message. Layer your chart, sequence your story, write your line.

Annotate in layers

Title → highlight → callout → takeaway. Four switches transform a silent chart into a slide that talks.

Use the 5-beat arc

Context → Question → Finding → So What → Recommendation. Never skip a beat — especially the last two.

Write the script first

Four sentences. We looked. We found. It matters. I recommend. Write it BEFORE you open the slide editor.

Adapt to the room

CEO wants impact. Engineers want cause. Marketing wants the soundbite. Same finding, three openings.

Earn your title

If your slide title is "Sales by month", you've left the story on the cutting-room floor. Make a claim, not a description.

One-sentence test

If you can't compress your finding into one tweetable line, you haven't found it yet. Keep digging.
